## 9.1

> **题目**　Let $\mathbb{G}$ be an abelian group. Prove that there is a unique identity in $\mathbb{G}$, and that every element $g \in \mathbb{G}$ has a unique inverse.

**Solution.**

Let $\mathbb{G}$ be a group, and let $e, e^{\prime} \in \mathbb{G}$ with $eg = ge = g$ and $e^{\prime}g = ge^{\prime} = g$ for all $g \in \mathbb{G}$. Then $e^{\prime} = e \cdot e^{\prime} = e$ and so $e$ and $e^{\prime}$ are the same element.

Let ${1}$ denote the identity in $\mathbb{G}$. Fix $g \in \mathbb{G}$ and let $h, h^{\prime} \in \mathbb{G}$ be such that $gh = hg = 1$ and $gh^{\prime} = h^{\prime}g = 1$. Then

$$hgh^{\prime} = (hg)h^{\prime} = 1 \cdot h^{\prime} = h^{\prime}$$

and

$$hgh^{\prime} = h(gh^{\prime}) = h \cdot 1 = h,$$

and so $h^{\prime} = h$.

## 9.2

> **题目**　Show that Proposition 9.36 does not necessarily hold when $\mathbb{G}$ is infinite.

**Solution.**

Take $\mathbb{G} = \mathbb{R}^+$ (i.e., the group of all positive real numbers) under multiplication. Let $\mathbb{H} = \{1\} \cup \{2, 4, 6, 8, \ldots\}$ be a subset of $\mathbb{G}$. Note that $\mathbb{H}$ satisfies the conditions of Proposition 9.36 but is not a subgroup since no elements in $\mathbb{H}$ (other than 1) have inverses in $\mathbb{H}$.

## 9.3

> **题目**　Let $\mathbb{G}$ be a finite group, and $g \in \mathbb{G}$. Show that $\langle g \rangle$ is a subgroup of $\mathbb{G}$. Is the set $\{g^0, g^1, \ldots\}$ necessarily a subgroup of $\mathbb{G}$ when $\mathbb{G}$ is infinite?

**Solution.**

We verify that $\langle g \rangle$ satisfies the conditions of Proposition 9.36. Since $g^0 = 1$, clearly $\langle g \rangle$ is nonempty. Furthermore, for any $a, b \in \langle g \rangle$ we know that there exist positive $x, y$ such that $a = g^x$ and $b = g^y$. But then $ab = g^{x+y} \in \langle g \rangle$. So $\langle g \rangle$ is a subgroup when $\mathbb{G}$ is finite.

The set $\{g^0, g^1, \ldots\}$ need not be a subgroup of $\mathbb{G}$ when $\mathbb{G}$ is infinite. For example, consider the group $\mathbb{R}^+$ of all positive real numbers with respect to multiplication. The set $S \stackrel{\mathrm{def}}{=} \{2^0, 2^1, 2^2, \ldots\}$ is not a subgroup of $\mathbb{R}^+$ since, for example, the element ${2} \in S$ has no inverse in $S$.

## 9.4

> **题目**　This question concerns the Euler phi function.
>
> (a) Let $p$ be a prime and $e \ge 1$ an integer. Show that
>
> $$\phi(p^e) = p^{e-1}(p-1).$$
>
> (b) Let $p, q$ be relatively prime. Show that $\phi(pq) = \phi(p) \cdot \phi(q)$. (You may use the Chinese remainder theorem.)
>
> (c) Prove Theorem 9.19.

**Solution.**

(a) The integers in $\{1, \ldots, p^e\}$ that are not relatively prime to $p$ are exactly the multiples of $p$, and there are exactly $p^e/p = p^{e-1}$ such integers. So $\phi(p^e) = p^e - p^{e-1} = p^{e-1}(p-1)$.

(b) We provide a solution that does not use the Chinese remainder theorem. Let $p$ and $q$ be relatively prime. Consider the integers $\{1, \ldots, pq\}$ arranged in an array as follows:

$$\begin{array}{cccc} 1 & p+1 & \cdots & (q-1)p+1 \\ 2 & p+2 & \cdots & (q-1)p+2 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ p & 2p & \cdots & qp \end{array}$$

For any $r \in \{1, \ldots, p\}$ having a factor in common with $p$, every element in the row

$$r \quad p+r \quad 2p+r \quad \cdots \quad (q-1)p+r$$

has a factor in common with $p$ and hence also has a factor in common with $pq$. Eliminate those rows from consideration, and consider the remaining $\phi(p)$ rows. Let

$$s \quad p+s \quad 2p+s \quad \cdots \quad (q-1)p+s$$

be any such a row (so $\gcd(s, p) = 1$). We claim that

$$[s \bmod q] \quad [p+s \bmod q] \quad [2p+s \bmod q] \quad \cdots \quad [(q-1)p+s \bmod q]$$

are the elements of $\mathbb{Z}_q$ in permuted order. This follows since there are $q$ such elements, and $ip + s = jp + s \bmod q$ implies $j = i$ (using $\gcd(p, q) = 1$). From this it follows that each remaining row contains exactly $\phi(q)$ elements relatively prime to $q$. The leaves a total of $\phi(pq) \stackrel{\mathrm{def}}{=} \phi(p) \cdot \phi(q)$ elements relatively prime to $pq$.【原文如此：The leaves】

(c) Let $N = \prod_i p_i^{e_i}$. Parts (a) and (b) give (using induction on $i$)

$$\phi(N) = \prod_i \phi(p_i^{e_1}) = \prod_i p_i^{e_i-1}(p_i - 1).$$

【原文如此：原文作 $p_i^{e_1}$，疑为 $p_i^{e_i}$ 之笔误】

## 9.5

> **题目**　Compute the final two (decimal) digits of ${3}^{1000}$ (by hand).

**Solution.**

Note that $|\mathbb{Z}_{100}^*| = \phi(100) = 40$. So

$$3^{1000} = 3^{[1000 \bmod 40]} = 3^0 = 1 \bmod 100.$$

## 9.6

> **题目**　Compute $[101^{4,800,000,002} \bmod 35]$ (by hand).

**Solution.**

Since $\phi(35) = 24$,

$$101^{4,800,000,002} = 101^{4,800,000,002 \bmod 24} = 101^2 = (-4)^2 = 16 \bmod 35.$$

Alternately, we can use the Chinese remainder theorem. First,

$$101^{4,800,000,002} = 1^{4,800,000,002} = 1 \bmod 5.$$

Next,

$$101^{4,800,000,002} = 3^{[4,800,000,002 \bmod 6]} = 3^2 = 2 \bmod 7.$$

Finally, we can explicitly compute (by trial-and-error) that $(1, 2) \leftrightarrow 16$.

## 9.7

> **题目**　Compute $[46^{51} \bmod 55]$ (by hand) using the Chinese remainder theorem.

**Solution.**

Since ${55} = 11 \cdot 5$, we work $\bmod 11$ and $\bmod 5$. First, $[46 \bmod 11] = 2$. Furthermore $\phi(11) = 10$ and thus

$$[46^{51} \bmod 11] = [2^{[51 \bmod 10]} \bmod 11] = [2^1 \bmod 11] = 2.$$

Similarly, $[46 \bmod 5] = 1$. We conclude that $[46^{51} \bmod 55] \leftrightarrow (2, 1)$. We thus need to find the value $x \in \mathbb{Z}_{55}$ such that $x \bmod 11 = 2$ and $x \bmod 5 = 1$. But we have already seen that ${46}$ is such a value. So $[46^{51} \bmod 55] = 46$.

## 9.8

> **题目**　Prove that if $\mathbb{G}, \mathbb{H}$ are groups, then $\mathbb{G} \times \mathbb{H}$ is a group.

**Solution.**

The conditions of Definition 9.9 are easy to verify. Closure and associativity are obvious. If $e, e^{\prime}$ are the identity elements of $\mathbb{G}$ and $\mathbb{H}$, respectively, then $(e, e^{\prime})$ is the identity of $\mathbb{G} \times \mathbb{H}$. Finally, any $(g, h) \in \mathbb{G} \times \mathbb{H}$ has inverse $(g^{-1}, h^{-1})$, where $g^{-1}$ (resp., $h^{-1}$) is the inverse of $g$ (resp., $h$) in $\mathbb{G}$ (resp., $\mathbb{H}$).

## 9.9

> **题目**　Let $p, N$ be integers with $p \mid N$. Prove that for any integer $X$,
>
> $$[[X \bmod N] \bmod p] = [X \bmod p].$$
>
> Show that, in contrast, $[[X \bmod p] \bmod N]$ need not equal $[X \bmod N]$.

**Solution.**

Let $b = [X \bmod N]$ so that $X$ can be written as $X = aN + b$ with $a \in \mathbb{Z}$. Let $\beta = [b \bmod p]$ so that $b$ can be written as $b = \alpha p + \beta$ with $\alpha \in \mathbb{Z}$. Then $X = aN + \alpha p + \beta$ and, since $p \mid N$, we therefore have $[X \bmod p] = \beta = [[X \bmod N] \bmod p]$, as desired.

On the other hand, let $X = 15$, $N = 10$, and $p = 5$. Then $[X \bmod N] = 5$ but $[[X \bmod p] \bmod N] = 0$.

## 9.12

> **题目**　This question concerns the group $\mathbb{Z}_{23}^*$.
>
> (a) What is the order of this group?
>
> (b) Compute $[3^{46} \bmod 23]$ (by hand).
>
> (c) Is this group cyclic? Is 2 a generator? What about 5?

**Solution.**

23 is prime, so $|\mathbb{Z}_{23}^*| = 23 - 1 = 22$. Then

$$3^{46} = 3^{[46 \bmod 22]} = 3^2 = 9 \bmod 23.$$

$\mathbb{Z}_p^*$ is always cyclic when $p$ is prime. Since

$$\langle 2 \rangle = \{1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 9, 18, 13, 3, 6, 12\},$$

we see that 2 is not a generator. On the other hand,

$$\langle 5 \rangle = \{1, 5, 2, 10, 4, 20, 8, 17, 16, 11, 9, 22, 18, 21, 13, 19, 3, 15, 6, 7, 12, 14\}$$

so 5 is a generator.

## 9.14

> **题目**　Corollary 9.21 shows that if $N = pq$ for distinct primes $p$ and $q$, and $ed = 1 \bmod \phi(N)$, then for all $x \in \mathbb{Z}_N^*$ we have $(x^e)^d = x \bmod N$. Show that this holds for all $x \in \{0, \ldots, N-1\}$.

**Solution.**

If $ed = 1 \bmod \phi(N)$ then, because $\phi(N) = (p-1)(q-1) = \phi(p) \cdot \phi(q)$, it also holds that $ed = 1 \bmod \phi(p)$. So for any $x \in \mathbb{Z}_p^*$ we know that $(x^e)^d = x \bmod p$. But for $x = 0$ it also holds, trivially, that $(x^e)^d = x \bmod p$. We conclude that for any $x \in \mathbb{Z}_p$, it holds that $(x^e)^d = x \bmod p$. The same holds with $p$ replaced by $q$.

Now let $x \in \mathbb{Z}_N$. Then $x \leftrightarrow (x_p, x_q)$ with $x_p \in \mathbb{Z}_p$ and $x_q \in \mathbb{Z}_q$. Now

$$\begin{aligned} (x^e)^d \ &\leftrightarrow \ ((x_p, x_q)^e)^d \\ &= ([(x_p^e)^d \bmod p], \ [(x_q^e)^d \bmod q]) \\ &= (x_p, x_q) \ \leftrightarrow \ x. \end{aligned}$$

## 9.15

> **题目**　Complete the details of the proof of the Chinese remainder theorem, showing that $\mathbb{Z}_N^*$ is isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}_p^* \times \mathbb{Z}_q^*$.

**Solution.**

Let $f$ be as defined in Theorem 9.24, restricted to domain $\mathbb{Z}_N^*$. It follows from the proof in the text that $f$ is a bijection. It therefore only remains to show that for any $a, b \in \mathbb{Z}_N^*$ we have $f(a \cdot_N b) = f(a) \boxdot f(b)$, where $\cdot_N$ denotes multiplication modulo $N$ and $\boxdot$ denotes the group operation in $\mathbb{Z}_p^* \times \mathbb{Z}_q^*$ (namely, multiplication modulo $p$ in the first component and modulo $q$ in the second component).

To see that this is true, note that

$$\begin{aligned} f(a \cdot_N b) &= ([(a \cdot_N b) \bmod p], \ [(a \cdot_N b) \bmod q]) \\ &= ([(a \cdot b) \bmod p], \ [(a \cdot b) \bmod q]) \\ &= ([a \bmod p], \ [a \bmod q]) \boxdot ([b \bmod p], \ [b \bmod q]) \\ &= f(a) \boxdot f(b). \end{aligned}$$

## 9.16

> **题目**　This exercise develops an efficient algorithm for testing whether an integer is a perfect power.
>
> (a) Show that if $N = \hat{N}^e$ for some integers $\hat{N}, e > 1$ then $e \le \|N\|$.
>
> (b) Given $N$ and $e$ with ${2} \le e \le \|N\|$, show how to determine in $\mathsf{poly}(\|N\|)$ time whether there exists an integer $\hat{N}$ with $\hat{N}^e = N$.
>
> (c) Given $N$, show how to test in $\mathsf{poly}(\|N\|)$ time whether $N$ is a perfect power.
>
> **Algorithm 9.1-S**
>
> **Computing** $N^{1/e}$
>
> **Input:** $N, e > 1$
>
> **Output:** $N^{1/e}$ if this is an integer; **false** otherwise
>
> $\mathbf{low} := 1$, $\mathbf{high} := N$
>
> **while** $\mathbf{low} \le \mathbf{high}$:
>
> - $\hat{N} := \lfloor (\mathbf{low} + \mathbf{high})/2 \rfloor$
> - **if** $\hat{N}^e = N$ then **return** $\hat{N}$ and halt
> - **if** $\hat{N}^e > N$ then $\mathbf{high} := \hat{N} - 1$
> - **if** $\hat{N}^e < N$ then $\mathbf{low} := \hat{N} + 1$
>
> **return** **false**

**Solution.**

(a) If $N = \hat{N}^e$ then $e = \log_{\hat{N}} N \le \log_2 N \le \|N\|$.

(b) Consider Algorithm 9.1-S. Clearly, the algorithm always outputs false when $N^{1/e}$ is not an integer. Otherwise, the algorithm outputs $N^{1/e}$ in at most $\mathcal{O}(\log N) = \mathcal{O}(\|N\|)$ iterations.

(c) Run Algorithm 9.1-S for each of at most $\mathcal{O}(\|N\|)$ values of $e$.

## 9.17

> **题目**　Given $N$ and $a \in \mathbb{Z}_N^*$, show how to test in polynomial time whether $a$ is a strong witness that $N$ is composite.

**Solution.**

See Algorithm 9.2-S.

**Algorithm 9.2-S** (Testing strong witnesses)

**Input:** odd integer $N$, $a \in \mathbb{Z}_N^*$

**Output:** Decide whether $a$ is a strong witness

Compute $r, u$ such that $N - 1 = 2^r u$ with $u$ odd.

$x := [a^u \bmod N]$

- **if** $x = \pm 1$ **then return** false
- **for** $i = 1$ **to** $r - 1$:
  - $x := [x^2 \bmod N]$
  - **if** $x = N - 1$ **then return** false
- **return** true

## 9.18

> **题目**　Fix $N, e$ with $\gcd(e, \phi(N)) = 1$, and assume there is an adversary $\mathcal{A}$ running in time $t$ for which
>
> $$\Pr[\mathcal{A}([x^e \bmod N]) = x] = 0.01,$$
>
> where the probability is taken over uniform choice of $x \in \mathbb{Z}_N^*$. Show that it is possible to construct an adversary $\mathcal{A}^{\prime}$ for which
>
> $$\Pr[\mathcal{A}^{\prime}([x^e \bmod N]) = x] = 0.99$$
>
> for *all* $x$. The running time $t^{\prime}$ of $\mathcal{A}^{\prime}$ should be polynomial in $t$ and $\|N\|$.

**Solution.**

Let $s$ be a parameter, fixed later. Construct $\mathcal{A}^{\prime}$ as follows:

**Algorithm $\mathcal{A}^{\prime}$**

On input $N, y, e$ do:

- (a) For $i = 1$ to $s$:
  - i. Choose $r_i \leftarrow \mathbb{Z}_N^*$.
  - ii. Run $\mathcal{A}([(r_i)^e \cdot y \bmod N])$ to obtain $x_i$.
  - iii. If $(x_i)^e = (r_i)^e \cdot y \bmod N$ then output $[x_i/r_i \bmod N]$ and terminate.
- (b) If the algorithm has not yet terminated, output fail.

Let $y$ be arbitrary. The key point to note is that, in every iteration, $\mathcal{A}$ is run on a *uniform* element of $\mathbb{Z}_N^*$, irrespective of how $y$ is distributed. This is so since $r_i$ is uniform, hence $[(r_i)^e \bmod N]$ is uniform (since raising to $e$th powers is a permutation), and thus $[(r_i)^e \cdot y \bmod N]$ is uniform (because $\mathbb{Z}_N^*$ is a group). Furthermore, if $\mathcal{A}$ ever correctly computes an $e$th root in any iteration, then $\mathcal{A}^{\prime}$ outputs the $e$th root of $y$: this follows since $(x_i)^e = (r_i)^e \cdot y \bmod N$ implies $(x_i/r_i)^e = y \bmod N$.

Combining the observations above and setting $s = 100 \ln 100$, we see that the probability that $\mathcal{A}^{\prime}$ *fails* to output an inverse is

$$\left(1 - \frac{1}{100}\right)^{100 \ln 100} = \left(\left(1 - \frac{1}{100}\right)^{100}\right)^{\ln 100} \le e^{-\ln 100} = \frac{1}{100}$$

(using Proposition A.2). The running time of $\mathcal{A}^{\prime}$ is $\mathcal{O}(t \cdot \mathsf{poly}(\|N\|))$.

## 9.19

> **题目**　Formally define the CDH assumption. Prove that hardness of the CDH problem relative to $\mathcal{G}$ implies hardness of the discrete-logarithm problem relative to $\mathcal{G}$, and that hardness of the DDH problem relative to $\mathcal{G}$ implies hardness of the CDH problem relative to $\mathcal{G}$.

**Solution.**

We first formally define what it means for the CDH problem to be hard.

The CDH experiment $\mathsf{CDH}_{\mathcal{A}, \mathcal{G}}(n)$:

- (a) Run $\mathcal{G}(1^n)$ to obtain $(\mathbb{G}, q, g)$, where $\mathbb{G}$ is a cyclic group of order $q$ (with $\|q\| = n$), and $g$ is a generator of $\mathbb{G}$.
- (b) Choose $h_1, h_2 \leftarrow \mathbb{G}$.
- (c) $\mathcal{A}$ is given $\mathbb{G}, q, g, h_1, h_2$, and outputs $h_3 \in \mathbb{G}$.
- (d) The output of the experiment is defined to be 1 if $h_3 = \mathsf{CDH}_g(h_1, h_2)$, and 0 otherwise.

As described, it is unclear how to test whether $h_3 = \mathsf{CDH}_g(h_1, h_2)$ efficiently. However, by choosing uniform $h_1$ with $\log_g h_1$ known (which can be done by choosing uniform $x_1 \in \mathbb{Z}_q$ and setting $h_1 := g^{x_1}$) it becomes easy to perform.

We say that the CDH problem is hard relative to $\mathcal{G}$ if for all probabilistic polynomial-time algorithms $\mathcal{A}$ there exists a negligible function $\mathsf{negl}$ such that

$$\Pr[\mathsf{CDH}_{\mathcal{A}, \mathcal{G}}(n) = 1] \le \mathsf{negl}(n).$$

Assume the CDH problem is hard relative to $\mathcal{G}$. Let $\mathcal{A}$ be a probabilistic polynomial-time algorithm and set $\varepsilon(n) \stackrel{\mathrm{def}}{=} \Pr[\mathsf{DLog}_{\mathcal{A}, \mathcal{G}}(n) = 1]$. We show how $\mathcal{A}$ can be used by a probabilistic polynomial-time algorithm $\mathcal{A}^{\prime}$ to solve the CDH problem with success probability $\varepsilon(n)$:

**Algorithm $\mathcal{A}^{\prime}$.** The algorithm is given $\mathbb{G}, q, g, h_1, h_2$ as input.

- (a) Run $\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{G}, q, g, h_1)$ and obtain output $x_1$.
- (b) Return $h_2^{x_1}$.

Observe that the input given to $\mathcal{A}$ when run as a subroutine by $\mathcal{A}^{\prime}$ is distributed exactly as in experiment $\mathsf{DLog}_{\mathcal{A}, \mathcal{G}}(n)$. So with probability $\varepsilon(n)$ it holds that $x_1 = \log_g h_1$. Whenever this occurs, $\mathcal{A}^{\prime}$ outputs the correct answer $\mathsf{CDH}_g(h_1, h_2)$. Since the CDH problem was assumed to be hard, this implies that $\varepsilon(n)$ is negligible and so the discrete logarithm problem must be hard as well.

We now prove that hardness of the DDH problem relative to $\mathcal{G}$ implies hardness of the CDH problem relative to $\mathcal{G}$. Let $\mathcal{A}$ be a probabilistic polynomial-time algorithm and set $\varepsilon(n) \stackrel{\mathrm{def}}{=} \Pr[\mathsf{CDH}_{\mathcal{A}, \mathcal{G}}(n) = 1]$. We show how $\mathcal{A}$ can be used by a probabilistic polynomial-time algorithm $\mathcal{A}^{\prime}$ to solve the DDH problem with roughly the same probability:

**Algorithm $\mathcal{A}^{\prime}$.** The algorithm is given $\mathbb{G}, q, g, h_1, h_2, h_3$ as input.

- (a) Run $\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{G}, q, g, h_1, h_2)$ and obtain output $h_3^{\prime}$.
- (b) If $h_3^{\prime} = h_3$ output 1; else output 0.

We need to analyze the probability that $\mathcal{A}^{\prime}$ outputs 1 when the final component of its input $h_3$ is equal to $\mathsf{CDH}_g(h_1, h_2)$, as compared to the probability that it outputs 1 when $h_3$ is chosen uniformly at random from $\mathbb{G}$. The input given to $\mathcal{A}$ when run as a subroutine by $\mathcal{A}^{\prime}$ is distributed exactly as in experiment $\mathsf{CDH}_{\mathcal{A}, \mathcal{G}}(n)$. So with probability $\varepsilon(n)$, it holds that $h_3^{\prime} = \mathsf{CDH}_g(h_1, h_2)$. It follows that when $h_3 = \mathsf{CDH}_g(h_1, h_2)$, algorithm $\mathcal{A}^{\prime}$ outputs 1 with probability at least $\varepsilon(n)$.

On the other hand, when $h_3$ is chosen uniformly at random from $\mathbb{G}$, then regardless of the behavior of $\mathcal{A}$ the probability that $h_3^{\prime} = h_3$ is exactly ${1}/|\mathbb{G}| = 1/q$. Thus,

$$\left|\Pr[\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(\mathbb{G}, q, g, g^x, g^y, g^z) = 1] - \Pr[\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(\mathbb{G}, q, g, g^x, g^y, g^{xy}) = 1]\right| \ge \varepsilon(n) - 1/q.$$

Since the above must be negligible by the assumption that the DDH problem is hard, and ${1}/q$ is negligible, it follows that $\varepsilon(n)$ must be negligible. We conclude that the CDH problem is hard as well.

## 9.21

> **题目**　Can the following problem be solved in polynomial time? Given a prime $p$, an integer $e \in \mathbb{Z}_{p-1}^*$, and $y := [g^e \bmod p]$ (where $g$ is a uniform value in $\mathbb{Z}_p^*$), find $g$, i.e., compute $y^{1/e} \bmod p$. If your answer is "yes," give a polynomial-time algorithm. If your answer is "no," show a reduction to one of the assumptions introduced in this chapter.

**Solution.**

The problem can be solved efficiently. Since $e \in \mathbb{Z}_{p-1}^*$ (and $p - 1$ is known), we can compute $d := [e^{-1} \bmod p - 1]$. Then

$$y^d = (g^e)^d = g^{ed} = g^{[ed \bmod p-1]} = g \bmod p.$$

## 9.24

> **题目**　When using the twisted Edwards representation, show that the inverse of a point $(x, y)$ is the point $(-x, y)$.

**Solution.**

(Note: we assume the twisted Edwards representation uses quadratic residue $a$ and quadratic non-residue $d$.) Using the addition law given in the text with $P_1 = (x_1, y_1) = (x, y)$ and $P_2 = (x_2, y_2) = (-x, y)$, we have that

$$P_1 + P_2 = \left( \frac{x_1 y_1 - x_1 y_1}{1 + d x_1 x_2 y_1 y_2}, \ \frac{y_1^2 + a x_1^2}{1 + d x_1^2 y_1^2} \right) = (0, 1),$$

using the fact that $P_1$ is on the curve so $a x_1^2 + y_1^2 = 1 + d x_1^2 y_1^2$. Since $(0, 1)$ is the identity, this completes the proof.

> **题目**　Consider the elliptic-curve group from Example 9.69. (See also Example 9.71.) Compute $(1,0)+(4,3)+(4,3)$ in this group by first converting to projective coordinates and then using Equations (9.4) and (9.5).

## 9.26

> **题目**　Fix $N$, an element $y \in \mathbb{Z}_N^*$, and $e$ with $\gcd(e, \phi(N)) = 1$. Show that given $w \in \mathbb{Z}_N^*$ and an integer $k$ with $\gcd(k, e) = 1$ and $w^e = y^k \bmod N$, it is possible to efficiently compute $x$ such that $x^e = y \bmod N$.

**Solution.**

Using the extended Euclidean algorithm (cf. Proposition 9.2), we can efficiently compute integers $X, Y$ such that $Xk + Ye = 1$. Then

$$y^1 = y^{Xk + Ye} = (y^k)^X (y^Y)^e = (w^X)^e (y^Y)^e = (w^X y^Y)^e \bmod N,$$

and so $[w^X y^Y \bmod N]$ (which can be efficiently computed since $X, Y$ are integers) is the $e$th root of $y$ modulo $N$.

## 9.27

> **题目**　Let $\mathsf{GenRSA}$ be as in Section 9.2.4. Prove that if the RSA problem is hard relative to $\mathsf{GenRSA}$ then Construction 9.80 is a fixed-length collision-resistant hash function.
>
> **Construction 9.80**.
>
> Define $(\mathsf{Gen}, H)$ as follows:
>
> - $\mathsf{Gen}$: on input ${1}^n$, run $\mathsf{GenRSA}(1^n)$ to obtain $N, e, d$, and select $y \leftarrow \mathbb{Z}_N^*$. The key is $s := \langle N, e, y \rangle$.
> - $H$: if $s = \langle N, e, y \rangle$, then $H^s$ maps inputs in $\{0,1\}^{3n}$ to outputs in $\mathbb{Z}_N^*$. Let $f_0^s(x) \stackrel{\mathrm{def}}{=} [x^e \bmod N]$ and $f_1^s(x) \stackrel{\mathrm{def}}{=} [y \cdot x^e \bmod N]$. For a ${3}n$-bit long string $x = x_1 \cdots x_{3n}$, define $H^s(x) \stackrel{\mathrm{def}}{=} f_{x_1}^s(f_{x_2}^s(\cdots(1)\cdots))$.

**Solution.**

We show that an $e$th root of $y$ can be computed from any collision; given this, a formal proof follows along the lines of the proof of Theorem 9.79.

Fix $s=\langle N,e,y\rangle$, and assume $y\neq1$ (computing an $e$th root of $y$ is trivial if $y=1$). Let $x,z$ be distinct inputs with $H^{s}(x)=H^{s}(z)$. Define $x^{0}=1$ and, for $i=1$ to ${3}n$, inductively define $x^{i}=f_{x_{i}}^{s}(x^{i-1})$ (note that $x^{1},\ldots,x^{3n}$ is the sequence of values produced when computing $H^{s}(x)$). Define $z^{i}$ analogously. Since $x\neq z$, there is some index $i$ with $x^{i}\neq z^{i}$ (in particular, take $i$ to be the first position where the strings $x$ and $z$ differ, i.e., where $x_{i}\neq z_{i}$). But since $x^{3n}=z^{3n}$, there must be an index $j<3n$ with

$$x^{j}\neq z^{j} \quad \text{but} \quad x^{j+1}=z^{j+1}.$$

Since $f_{0}^{s}$, $f_{1}^{s}$ are permutations (this can be easily verified), it must be the case that $x_{j+1} \neq z_{j+1}$. Assume without loss of generality that $x_{j+1} = 0$. The above shows that given a collision we can efficiently compute distinct elements $X, Z \in \mathbb{Z}_N^*$ with $f_0^s(X) = f_1^s(Z)$. Then

$$X^{e}=y\cdot Z^{e}\bmod N,$$

and we see that $[X/Z \bmod N]$ is an $e$th root of $y$.

## 9.28

> **题目**　Consider the following generalization of Construction 9.78:
>
> **Construction 9.2-S**.
>
> Define a fixed-length hash function $(\mathsf{Gen}, H)$ as follows:
>
> - $\mathsf{Gen}$: on input ${1}^n$, run $\mathcal{G}(1^n)$ to obtain $(\mathbb{G}, q, h_1)$ and then select $h_2, \ldots, h_t \leftarrow \mathbb{G}$. Output $s := \langle \mathbb{G}, q, (h_1, \ldots, h_t) \rangle$ as the key.
> - $H$: given a key $s = \langle \mathbb{G}, q, (h_1, \ldots, h_t) \rangle$ and input $(x_1, \ldots, x_t)$ with $x_i \in \mathbb{Z}_q$, output $H^s(x_1, \ldots, x_t) := \prod_i h_i^{x_i}$.
>
> (a) Prove that if the discrete logarithm problem is hard relative to $\mathcal{G}$, and $q$ is prime, then for any $t = \mathsf{poly}(n)$ this construction is a fixed-length collision-resistant hash function.
>
> (b) Discuss how this construction can be used to obtain *compression* regardless of the number of bits needed to represent elements of $\mathbb{G}$ (as long as it is polynomial in $n$).

**Solution.**

(a) The easiest way to prove this is by reducing to the security of Construction 9.78. That is, we show that any algorithm $\mathcal{A}$ finding a collision in Construction 9.2-S can be used as a subroutine to find collisions in Construction 9.78.

Let $\Pi = (\mathsf{Gen}, H)$ denote Construction 9.2-S and let $\hat{\Pi} = (\hat{\mathsf{Gen}}, \hat{H})$ denote Construction 9.78. Given a probabilistic polynomial-time algorithm $\mathcal{A}$ attacking $\Pi$, let $\varepsilon(n) \stackrel{\mathrm{def}}{=} \Pr[\mathsf{Hash-coll}_{\mathcal{A}, \Pi}(n) = 1]$. Construct algorithm $\hat{\mathcal{A}}$ attacking $\hat{\Pi}$ as follows:

**Algorithm $\hat{\mathcal{A}}$:**

The algorithm is given $\hat{s} = \langle \mathbb{G}, q, g, h \rangle$ as input.

- i. Choose $i \leftarrow \{1, \ldots, t\}$ and set $h_i := h$.
- ii. For $j \in \{1, \ldots, t\} \setminus \{i\}$, choose $\beta_j \leftarrow \mathbb{Z}_q$ and set $h_j := g^{\beta_j}$. Set $s := \langle \mathbb{G}, q, (h_1, \ldots, h_t) \rangle$.
- iii. Run $\mathcal{A}(s)$ and obtain outputs $(x_1, \ldots, x_t)$ and $(x_1^{\prime}, \ldots, x_t^{\prime})$.
- iv. If $x_i \ne x_i^{\prime}$ then
  - A. Set $X_1 := \sum_{j \ne i} \beta_j \cdot x_j$ and $X_2 := x_i$.
  - B. Set $X_1^{\prime} := \sum_{j \ne i} \beta_j \cdot x_j^{\prime}$ and $X_2^{\prime} := x_i^{\prime}$.
- v. Output $(X_1, X_2)$ and $(X_1^{\prime}, X_2^{\prime})$.

We first claim that whenever $(x_1, \ldots, x_t)$ and $(x_1^{\prime}, \ldots, x_t^{\prime})$ form a collision in $H^s$ with $x_i \ne x_i^{\prime}$, then $\hat{\mathcal{A}}$ outputs a collision in $\hat{H}^{\hat{s}}$. To see this, note that

$$\begin{aligned} H^s(x_1, \ldots, x_t) &= \prod_j h_j^{x_j} \\ &= h^{x_i} \cdot \prod_{j \ne i} (g^{\beta_j})^{x_j} \\ &= h^{x_i} \cdot g^{\sum_{j \ne i} \beta_j \cdot x_j} \\ &= g^{X_1} \cdot h^{X_2} \ = \ \hat{H}^{\hat{s}}(X_1, \ X_2), \end{aligned}$$

and similarly $H^s(x_1^{\prime}, \ldots, x_t^{\prime}) = \hat{H}^{\hat{s}}(X_1^{\prime}, X_2^{\prime})$. Since the values output by $\mathcal{A}$ collide, $\hat{H}^{\hat{s}}(X_1, X_2) = \hat{H}^{\hat{s}}(X_1^{\prime}, X_2^{\prime})$; since $x_i \ne x_i^{\prime}$ we have $X_1^{\prime} \ne X_2^{\prime}$ and so $(X_1, X_2)$ and $(X_1^{\prime}, X_2^{\prime})$ are a collision in $\hat{H}^{\hat{s}}$.

We now analyze the probability that $\hat{\mathcal{A}}$ outputs a collision. First note that the distribution on the inputs given to $\mathcal{A}$ when run as a subroutine by $\hat{\mathcal{A}}$ is identical to the distribution in experiment $\mathsf{Hash-coll}_{\mathcal{A}, \Pi}(n)$, and $\mathcal{A}$ therefore outputs a collision $(x_1, \ldots, x_t)$, $(x_1^{\prime}, \ldots, x_t^{\prime})$ with probability exactly $\varepsilon(n)$. For at least[^1] one value $k \in \{1, \ldots, t\}$ we have $x_k \ne x_k^{\prime}$. $\mathcal{A}$ has no information about $\hat{\mathcal{A}}$'s choice of $i$ since each element $h_1, \ldots, h_t$ is a uniform element of $\mathbb{G}$. It follows that $x_i \ne x_i^{\prime}$ with probability at least ${1}/t$. Combining this with the argument given earlier, we see that $\hat{\mathcal{A}}$ outputs a collision with probability at least $\varepsilon(n)/t$. Since this must be negligible (by security of $\hat{\Pi}$) and $t = \mathsf{poly}(n)$, it follows that $\varepsilon(n)$ must be negligible as well.

(b) The input length of Construction 9.2-S can be $t \cdot (n-1)$ bits (since $\|q\| = n$), and the output length is the length of a bit-string needed to represent an element of $\mathbb{G}$. The latter is $p(n)$ for some polynomial $p$. Setting $t > p(n)/(n-1)$, we obtain compression.

[^1]: Actually, there must be at least two such values.
